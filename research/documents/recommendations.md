# Recommendations

The agent does not write letters of recommendation. It helps the person choose writers and prepare a packet those writers can use.

## Findings

- **Letters are the material doctoral programs most often named at the final decision** (67%), ahead of the statement (64%) and transcripts (55%). They are already important at the first screen (57%). [S06, B]
- **Across studies, letters correlate weakly but positively with later grades, ratings, productivity, and finishing.** Their clearest incremental contribution, beyond the usual numbers, is for finishing the degree. [S04, A]
- **In one biomedical program, the enthusiasm of the ratings (not the interview) tracked later papers.** Students with three or more first-author papers had stronger average recommender ratings than students with no first-author paper and only a middle-author paper. Interview scores did not separate those groups. Letter writers had watched the person work; interviewers had about 30 minutes. [S03, B]
- **Letters are still biased and inflated.** Almost every letter for an admitted student is positive. The useful signal in the UNC study was which top adjective the writer picked, not the existence of praise. [S03, B]

## What the agent does

- Recommend writers who supervised the person's research or a substantial project, in this order: research advisor, thesis supervisor, then a faculty member who taught a methods course and saw the person's work. A famous name who does not know the work goes below a less famous writer who does. [S03, B; S04, A]
- Three letters is the usual ask. If the program sets a number, use that.
- Write `custom_resumes/<slug>/recommender_notes.md`: for each writer, the projects they saw, the dates, and two or three true contributions the person hopes the letter can discuss. Facts only from the master.
- Never draft the letter itself, never invent a writer's opinion, and never tell the person to sign a writer's name.
- If the person has no one who has seen them do research, say so in the match report. Do not paper over it with a letter from a family friend or an employer who only saw shift work, unless that job contained real research and the program accepts professional letters.
